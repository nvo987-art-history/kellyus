Visual Identity / Cultural Systems

The Evolution of the Kelly Code

From Identity to Cultural Systems

V1–V5
Nicholas Van-Orton

Identity as an organizing logic

A conceptual trajectory from individual recognizability toward systemic cultural operation.

00 Introduction

The question of continuity

Visual identity often appears recognizable at first glance, while the principles underlying its operation are far more complex than can be defined by a few characteristic forms, colors, or objects. The carrier of an identity may change, move into a new medium, assume a different social role, or even become part of a larger cultural system, while still retaining a recognizable continuity. The different versions of the Kelly Code examine this problem on an increasingly broader scale. Their central question is what makes identity recognizable amid change, and at what point that identity develops from a phenomenon tied to a person or object into an autonomous cultural system.

01 Kelly Code V1

Portability

The starting point of Kelly Code V1 is that the persistence of identity is not synonymous with permanence. An identity proves strong when its organizing logic can be carried forward across different situations. In Sonia Delaunay's case, the transition from painting toward textiles and fashion makes this portability visible. Color, geometry, formal rhythm, and visual dynamism do not remain confined to a single medium but continue to operate in new forms of appearance. Recognizability therefore does not arise from the repetition of a specific object or a single visual sign, but from the fact that certain relationships and organizational principles can be recreated across different carriers.

Grace Kelly's example makes the same question examinable through a change in social role. The role of the Hollywood film star is replaced by that of the Princess of Monaco and a diplomatic figure, while discipline, dignity, control, and elegance continue to function as organizing elements of identity.

From this perspective, it is also worth examining a scene from To Catch a Thief (1955), when Grace Kelly and Cary Grant are in a room, while fireworks flash in the background. The camera here does not merely capture a romantic moment, but also a consciously composed visual message: Grace Kelly's gaze is controlled, her presence tight and measured. The dynamism of the fireworks flashing behind her does not “outshine” her, but on the contrary: frames and amplifies the fact that she herself is calm and disciplined. The lines of the dress, the accessories, the posture, and the rhythm all function as signs – every element of the “Kelly Code” is in its place. This is no longer simple elegance, but conscious visual editing: a perfectly constructed visual identity, with the same precision as Sonia Delaunay placed colors on the canvas.

The change is therefore significant, yet it does not necessarily interrupt the continuity of identity. V1 consequently interprets the visual code not as a collection of isolated characteristics, but as a system of recurring relationships capable of maintaining recognizability under changing conditions.

02 Kelly Code V2

Emergent meaning

This insight leads to Kelly Code V2, which no longer asks primarily how an identity persists across changing carriers, but how visual meaning itself comes into being. The basic premise of V2 is that elements do not necessarily carry their meaning independently: their meaning also emerges from the ways in which they relate to one another. In Sonia Delaunay's painting, therefore, it is not merely the individual fields of color or forms that matter, but their interaction. Visual structure becomes perceptible through the relationships formed between them.

A similar organizing principle appears in the choreography of Rosalía’s performance “Berghain (Live at The BRIT Awards 2026) ft. Björk,” where the relationship between bodies and groups creates a perceptible structure. A single body or movement does not necessarily determine the meaning of the composition as a whole. The structure emerges from the ways in which the individual elements repeat, align with one another, diverge from one another, and are rearranged. The repetition of these relationships produces a visual rhythm that keeps the viewer’s attention in continuous motion.

The power of suspense comes from the fact that attention is not static. It continuously moves forward, searching for relationships, evaluating possibilities, and trying to recognize patterns. The viewer becomes not merely an observer, but an active participant in the system.

The same logic can be found in Delaunay's work. The eye never settles on a single point. Relationships between colors keep it moving, leading it toward new connections before returning it to earlier elements. The meaning of the image is not located in any single form, but in the pathway of attention.

A similar principle can also be observed in Hitchcock's cinema, where suspense depends on the controlled movement of attention. The viewer's attention is directed toward certain details, relationships, and possibilities while other elements remain temporarily unresolved. Meaning and tension therefore emerge not only from what is shown, but from the sequence in which visual information becomes perceptible and from the relationships the viewer is led to establish between different elements.

For the Kelly Code, this provided another confirmation. Identity is not simply a collection of characteristics, but a pattern that repeatedly directs attention toward the same relationships and meanings.

Strong identities are therefore not merely recognizable—they also guide attention. Just as a well-composed painting or a Hitchcock scene creates a structured experience, the strongest identities create consistent pathways for perception. Behind their different expressions, the same rhythm, the same organizing principle, and the same pattern return again and again.

This direction of attention can dissolve the existence of a single stable center. The composition may therefore function not as a hierarchical system in which every element points toward one central meaning, but as a decentralized structure that is continuously reorganized. Kelly Code V2 consequently foregrounds the problem of emergent meaning: meaning arises from the dynamics and rhythmic organization of relationships.

03 Kelly Code V3

Cultural stabilization

Kelly Code V3 expands this idea further by examining how a visual identity can become a stable cultural system across different media, spaces, and forms of appearance. In the case of Jacqueline Kennedy, the White House, the media, clothing, cultural representation, and public presence appear not as isolated domains, but as elements of an interconnected visual system. In Sonia Delaunay's case, color, geometry, textiles, clothing, and interior space are connected in a similar way: they can be understood as different fields of manifestation of the same organizing logic.

The question of V3 is therefore whether a consistent visual language can create a network of relationships capable of arranging different appearances into a single, culturally recognizable system. Within this system, the concepts of Controlled Visibility and Curated Presence acquire particular significance. The power of presence is shaped not only by the degree of visibility, but also by the regulation of access and the coordination of appearances. A visual language stabilized in this way may, over time, become a cultural Reference Point: a point of orientation to which the interpretation of other phenomena can also be connected.

04 Kelly Code V4

Autonomy

Kelly Code V4 moves the question to a new level by making autonomy, rather than recognizability, its central problem. An identity may be recognizable without having become a genuinely autonomous system; its meaning may still be determined by a previous environment, collective, or relationship. V4 therefore examines when an identity becomes capable of maintaining its own operational framework independently of the system from which it emerged.

The example of Sonia and Robert Delaunay makes this problem examinable through the relationship between two identities connected to the same artistic system. Despite their shared artistic environment, the question arises whether one identity can become an autonomous cultural system that no longer derives its intelligibility exclusively from the shared framework. Sonia Delaunay's presence beyond painting—in fashion, textile design, and other visual fields—makes this movement toward autonomy visible.

The example of Beyoncé and Destiny's Child applies the same question to the relationship between collective and individual identity. The essential issue is not simply whether a person can emerge from a collective system, but whether, after being connected to that earlier system, they can establish an independent identity system of their own. V4 thus draws a clear distinction between recognizability and autonomy: the former concerns continuity, while the latter concerns the conditions of independent operation.

05 Kelly Code V5

The era as identity structure

This conceptual trajectory leads to Kelly Code V5, which examines the question of visual and cultural identity on the scale of an entire era. The 1980s, as a problem, raise the question of what systems make it possible for a whole historical and cultural period to become recognizable. The examination of the 1980s therefore shifts attention from the question of individual identity toward cultural relationships, repetitions, and organizational patterns that extend beyond individual persons.

06 Conclusion

From identity to systems

The Kelly Code proposes that identity should be understood not as a set of fixed characteristics, but as an organizing structure whose recognizability may persist, change, or weaken depending on the relationships through which it operates. Across its different levels, the framework moves from the portability of identity through the emergence of meaning, cultural stabilization, and autonomy, ultimately reaching the recognizability of an entire era as a structured cultural configuration.

The progression from V1 to V5 therefore does not represent five separate theories, but five analytical scales of the same problem. What initially appears as the identity of an individual or object can, through relationships, repetition, visibility, and cultural circulation, become part of a broader system. At the highest level, the question is no longer what makes a person or object recognizable, but how the recurring organization of identities and cultural practices makes an entire era recognizable.

The Kelly Code therefore takes into account both continuity and disruption. An identity may persist despite a change in medium, role, or context, while at the same time its recognizability may depend on relationships and systems that cannot simply be carried forward. This tension is not a limitation of the framework, but one of its central analytical conditions: it examines identity precisely where continuity meets change.
